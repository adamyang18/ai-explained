Quantization, distillation, small models:
fitting an elephant in a fridge

How to make models cheaper and faster, and even small enough to fit on a phone.

Panel 1 · Why go small

Big models are pricey, slow, and hard to move

Three ways to shrink a model: quantization (less precision), distillation (a teacher trains a student), and using a small model as a specialist.

Panel 2 · Quantization

Each knob keeps fewer decimal places

7 billion × 2 bytes ≈ 14 GB; 7 billion × 0.5 byte ≈ 3.5 GB. It shrinks to a quarter of the size, and usually only gets a little worse.

Think of it like

Saving a photo as a JPG. Squeeze a 20 MB original down to 2 MB and you can barely tell the difference. Only when you squeeze too hard do you get blocky pixels.

What’s really going on

Quantization doesn’t need retraining. You just “compress” a model that’s already trained, which is why, soon after an open-weights model comes out, the community posts all kinds of quantized versions. Real files end up a bit bigger than this estimate because of extra metadata.

Common myth

“The quantized version is just as good as the original.” For everyday chat the difference is small, but precise work like math, code and long reasoning drops more noticeably. Test it on your own exam before you ship.

Panel 3 · Distillation

The teacher writes out answers, the student learns from them

It’s much faster than learning from scratch, because the teacher gives more than answers. It shows how to solve the problem.

Example

When DeepSeek released R1, it also released a batch of “distilled” versions: it used reasoning data generated by R1 to train small models built on Qwen and Llama, giving those small models some reasoning ability too.

What’s really going on

A distilled student is usually lopsided: great at the problem types the teacher showed it, weak at the ones it didn’t. So you pick the practice problems to match what you actually need.

Heads up

Many closed models’ terms of use ban using their output to train a competing model. Before distilling, check the teacher model’s license.

Panel 4 · Small models as specialists

You don’t need a professor to sort the mail

The split is illustrative. The idea is to route by difficulty: the bill can drop a lot while quality barely moves.

Remember this

  1. Quantization: knobs keep fewer decimal places, the model gets several times smaller, quality drops a little
  2. Distillation: the big model writes out problems and reasoning, the small model learns from them
  3. Small models: specialists that handle simple, frequent jobs
  4. Routing: split by difficulty; mixing big and small models saves the most
  5. Whenever you shrink a model, test it on your own exam before it goes live
