RAG and Embeddings:
making AI look things up before it talks

The most common way companies put AI to work, and the basic idea behind how AI search cites web pages.

Part 1 · EmbeddingsFirst, learn to “find things by meaning”
PICTURE 1 · TEXT BECOMES COORDINATES

Every piece of text gets a spot on a “meaning map”

Turn text into a list of numbers (coordinates), and that list is called an embedding (a vector).

Think of it like…

Grocery store aisles. Milk sits next to yogurt, shampoo sits next to body wash. You don't need to know a product's name; walk to the right aisle and you'll find similar stuff.

How it actually works

A special embedding model reads a piece of text and outputs a long list of numbers. It also learned from huge amounts of text: things that tend to show up in similar contexts get placed close together.

Common myth

“A vector is just keywords.” Nope. It captures meaning. The same word can land in different spots in different sentences, like “an Apple phone” versus “a tasty apple.”

PICTURE 2 · SEARCH BY MEANING

Not a single word in common, and it still finds it

Old-school search needs the words to match. Vector search needs the meaning to match: it measures the distance between two points.

Think of it like…

Telling a store clerk “I want shoes that don't get sweaty in winter.” A good clerk walks you straight to the wool shoes.

How it actually works

The question and all the documents are turned into coordinates ahead of time and stored in a vector database. When you ask, it measures distances and grabs the closest few. Real systems often mix keyword search and vector search together; that's called “hybrid search.”

Common myth

“Vector search always beats keyword search.” For exact strings like model numbers, names or order numbers, keyword search is actually more accurate.

Part 2 · RAGPut “finding the info” and “writing the answer” together
PICTURE 3 · WHY WE NEED IT

Three things the model doesn't know

Instead of making it answer from memory, put the material right in its hands.

PICTURE 4 · RAG IN THREE STEPS

Find → hand over → answer

A closed-book test becomes an open-book test. Same model, it just has reference material at hand.

Think of it like…

A lawyer's assistant goes to the file room, pulls three relevant case files, and puts them on the lawyer's desk. The lawyer reads them, then gives advice.

How it actually works

Step ② is usually a fixed set of instructions, like: “Answer only from the material below. If it's not in there, say you don't know. Mark each sentence with its source number.”

Common myth

“With RAG, it won't hallucinate.” It'll happen a lot less, but it can still misread the material, mash two sources together wrong, or fill in gaps itself when the material isn't enough.

PICTURE 5 · CHUNKING

Long documents get cut into small chunks first

Each chunk gets its own coordinates and gets found on its own. How well you chunk decides how well it finds things.

Think of it like…

Breaking a book into index cards. One card, one idea: quick to find. One card stuffed with three ideas: you'll keep finding stuff that's only half right.

How it actually works

A common approach is to cut by paragraph or heading, with a little overlap between neighboring chunks so no sentence gets sliced in half. Each chunk also gets tags like title, source and date, which help with filtering and citing.

Common myth

“Just dump the documents in and you're done.” When RAG works badly, it's usually not the model's fault. It's messy material, bad chunking, or old content nobody cleaned out.

PICTURE 7 · AI SEARCH

Why ChatGPT cites some websites and not others

AI search is basically RAG with web access. A page gets cited if it can be found in step ① and if one of its chunks stands on its own well enough to be quoted in step ③. (Making content good at this has a name: GEO.)

Think of it like…

The lawyer's assistant pulling case files again. A file has to be in the file room first, and labeled clearly, before the assistant picks it and the lawyer quotes it.

What gets cited

One paragraph, one idea. The main point right up front. Numbers, names and dates spelled out clearly. That way, even if a chunk gets cut out on its own, it still makes sense.

For school: when an AI answer cites sources, click them. That's how you check it before you use it in an assignment.

Heads up

Exactly how each AI search finds and ranks pages isn't public. This is the general idea they share, not any one company's rules.

The short version

  1. Embedding: turns text into coordinates on a “meaning map.” Similar meaning = close together
  2. Vector search: finds material by meaning, not by matching words
  3. RAG: look up the material, hand it to the model, then have it answer from that material
  4. How good RAG is depends half on the model, half on the material and how it's chunked
  5. AI search = RAG with web access. Content gets cited when it can be found and a chunk makes sense on its own
