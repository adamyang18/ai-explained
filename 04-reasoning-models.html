Reasoning Models:
Scratch Work First, Then the Answer

Spend more time "thinking" to get more answers right.

Panel 1 · What's the difference

A regular model blurts out an answer. A reasoning model works it out first.

Before answering, a reasoning model writes scratch work just for itself: it breaks the problem into steps, double-checks, and fixes mistakes it spots.

Panel 2 · What's really going on

"Thinking" is still next-word prediction, with scratch work written first

Same as lesson one: it still writes one token at a time. It just writes the scratch work first, then the answer.

Think of it like

On a math test, you have to show your work on scrap paper. You're the same person. You just aren't allowed to do it in your head and jump to the answer, so you make fewer mistakes.

How it actually works

Early on, people found that adding "think step by step" to a prompt made answers more accurate. That's called "chain of thought." A reasoning model has that habit trained right into it.

Common myth

"The scratch work is exactly what's going on inside the model." The scratch work helps it get better answers, but it doesn't necessarily give a complete or honest picture of how the model really reached its answer.

Panel 3 · How it's trained

Practice on problems with right answers, get rewarded for getting them right

That's why reasoning models have improved the most in areas where answers can be checked as right or wrong, like math, coding and logic.

Panel 4 · Trading time for accuracy

Thinking longer gets better answers, but it's worth less and less

When an app lets you pick a thinking level (low / medium / high), you're choosing where to sit on this curve.

Think of it like

In chess you can spend 10 seconds or 10 minutes on a move. Thinking longer really does help, but going from 5 minutes to 10 doesn't add much.

How it actually works

The industry calls this "test-time compute." Models used to get stronger mainly by using more computing power during training. Now there's a second path: use more computing power while answering.

Common myth

"Always turn thinking all the way up." Thinking longer on an easy question doesn't help. Sometimes it "overthinks" and changes a right answer to a wrong one, and it's slower and more expensive too.

Panel 5 · When to use it

Turn thinking on for hard problems, off for easy ones

Good for thinking modeNo need for thinking mode
Multi-step math, financial calculationsTranslating, rewriting, polishing
Writing and debugging complex codeSimple Q&A, summarizing what you looked up
Planning and scheduling with lots of conditionsCommon customer-service questions
Breaking down complex agent tasksLive chats that need instant replies

A common approach at companies: start in regular mode, and only step up for hard problems. It saves money without hurting results.

Background

A new wave that started in 2024

DeepSeek-R1 mattered because it made its weights and methods public, so "reasoning" was no longer something only a few closed-model companies had.

Remember it in one line each

  1. Reasoning model = writes scratch work for itself before it answers
  2. Trained with reinforcement learning: practice on problems with right answers, get points for getting them right
  3. More thinking = more accurate, but slower and pricier, with shrinking returns
  4. Turn thinking on for hard problems, off for easy ones
