Evals:
Giving AI a Test

When a company decides whether to launch an AI tool, this test score is what it looks at.

Panel 1 · Why test at all

"I tried it a few times and it seemed fine" doesn't count

AI can answer differently each time, and it breaks in places you'd never expect. Only testing it on lots of questions shows how good it really is. Same reason you don't judge a pitcher on five pitches: you look at the whole game log.

Panel 2 · Three ways to grade

Who marks the test?

The grading rules should be specific things you can check, like "Did it quote the source?" or "Did it mention the refund deadline?"

Think of it like

A machine marks the multiple choice; the teacher marks the essays. When there are too many essays, a TA marks them using the rubric, and the teacher spot-checks whether the TA got it right.

How it actually works

Using a model as the judge is called "LLM-as-a-judge." You give the judge a rubric and a few already-graded examples, and it returns a score plus its reasons. Before trusting it, you run it on a batch of questions people already graded and see how often it agrees with them.

Watch out for AI judges

Common biases: they like longer answers, they like whichever option comes first, and they like answers that sound like themselves. When comparing two answers, swap the order and test both ways.

Panel 3 · Where the questions come from

The best questions come from real work

This set of questions is often called a "golden dataset": the questions plus agreed-on good answers or grading rules.

Think of it like

A driving test doesn't just check driving in a straight line. It specifically tests parallel parking and starting on a hill. Wherever people usually mess up is exactly what the test should cover.

How it actually works

You can start with a few dozen questions. No need to wait until you have thousands. What matters is that they reflect how the tool is really used, and that someone who knows the work signs off on "what counts as a good answer."

Common myth

"Just have the AI write its own test." You can use it to add more questions, but if you only test it on questions it can think of, you'll never find the blind spots it can't.

Panel 4 · Public leaderboards vs. your own test

Top of the class doesn't mean good at your job

Public leaderboards (benchmarks) are good for a first cut. The final call should come from your own test.

Think of it like

When hiring, a degree gets someone through the first round, but what decides it is the interview and how they actually do on your team.

How it actually works

Public benchmark questions have been out there for so long that they may have leaked into a model's training data, which inflates its score. That's called "data contamination." And every AI company shows off the leaderboards that make it look best, so they're hard to compare directly.

Common myth

"Pick whichever model is #1 on the leaderboard and you're safe." For a lot of real work, a cheap small model with good RAG and good prompts does just as well as a big one on your own test.

Panel 5 · What subjects to test

It's not just "right or wrong"

SubjectThe question
AccuracyIs the answer right?
Backed by sourcesWhen using RAG, can every sentence be traced back to the documents? Did it make anything up?
Completion rateHow often does an agent finish its task from start to end? How many steps does it take on average?
Safety and rulesDid it refuse what it should refuse? Did it leak anything it shouldn't?
Format and toneDoes it follow the company's style? Is it pleasant for customers to read?
Speed and costHow long does it take? How many tokens, and how much money, per answer?

Which subjects matter most depends on the job. Customer service cares most about tone and accuracy; finance cares most about accuracy and following the rules.

Panel 6 · Change something, test again

The test is your progress dashboard

Without a test, every change is a guess. With one, you can see right away whether each change helped, and you'll catch it the moment something gets worse.

Think of it like

Weighing yourself every day while trying to lose weight. You could skip it, but then you'd never know which change actually worked, or notice when the weight started creeping back.

How it actually works

The test gets plugged into an automatic pipeline: after you change a prompt, swap models or update the documents, it re-runs on its own and raises an alarm if the score drops. In software this is called "regression testing." When the AI company releases a new version of its model, you re-run it too.

Common myth

"Testing once before launch is enough." Real users keep coming up with new situations. You need to keep grading samples and add every new failure to the test.

Panel 7 · The business view

Evals are the shared language between buyer and seller

For a client, a report card based on their own data is more convincing than any demo.

Remember it in one line each

  1. Eval = a fixed set of test questions used to check how well an AI performs, at scale
  2. Grading: code checks the answer, people score it, or an AI judges it. Usually a mix
  3. The best questions come from real work, plus tricky ones and a mistake log
  4. Public leaderboards are a first cut; your own test makes the call
  5. Re-test after every change, and keep spot-checking after launch
  6. Before a pilot, agree with the client on what score counts as a pass
