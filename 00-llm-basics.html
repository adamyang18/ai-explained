A large language model
is a next-word champion

8 pictures that show what AIs like ChatGPT and Claude are actually doing. Under each one: an analogy, how it really works, and a common myth.

PICTURE 1 · THE ONE TRICK

It does one thing: guess the next word

That long answer you see? It was built one word at a time.

Think of it like…

The next-word suggestions on your phone keyboard. The difference: it has read a ridiculous amount of text, and it can take tens of thousands of earlier words into account before suggesting the next one.

How it actually works

At every step it gives a score (a probability) to every block in its “dictionary,” picks one, adds it on, then re-reads the whole thing including the new word to guess the next one.

It doesn't plan the whole answer before it starts. So-called “thinking mode” just has it write a rough draft for itself first, then write the real answer. Still next-word guessing, all the way down.

Common myth

“It only guesses words, so it's dumb.” To guess the next word well, you have to understand grammar, facts, logic, even how code will run. Trying to “guess well” forced out a lot of abilities that look a lot like understanding.

PICTURE 2 · TOKENS

Text gets chopped into little building blocks

The model doesn't know “words.” It only knows block numbers. And it counts everything in blocks: the longer the chat, the more blocks.

Think of it like…

LEGO. A common word is one ready-made piece. A rare word gets built from a few smaller pieces.

How it actually works

Rule of thumb for English: 1 token ≈ 4 letters ≈ ¾ of a word. A Chinese character often takes 1–2 tokens each, depending on the model.

Pricing, length limits and speed are all counted in tokens. The same idea can cost more “blocks” in some languages than others.

Common myth

“It can see every letter.” It sees block numbers, not letters. That's why questions like “how many r's are in strawberry?” used to trip up early models.

PICTURE 3 · TRAINING

First, it reads an entire library

Training means: hide the next word, make it guess, and adjust it when it's wrong. After an astronomical number of rounds, it has picked up grammar, common knowledge and how people talk.

Think of it like…

A student who does trillions of fill-in-the-blank questions. No teacher, no lessons. After each one, they just check against the original text.

How it actually works

This is called “self-supervised” learning: the answer is just the next word in the original text, so nobody has to label anything by hand. That's why it can use such huge amounts of text.

Training runs on lots of GPUs for weeks to months. When it's done, the knowledge is “frozen.” So every model has a knowledge cutoff date. It doesn't know what happened after that, unless it's hooked up to web search.

Common myth

“It saved all the websites and looks things up when it answers.” Nope. There's no database of the original text inside. What it learned are patterns, squeezed into its parameters. A few bits of text that showed up many, many times, it might be able to recite.

PICTURE 4 · PARAMETERS

What it learns is stored in a pile of tiny knobs

Every wrong guess nudges these knobs a tiny bit. More knobs means it can capture finer patterns.

Think of it like…

A giant mixing board. One knob on its own means nothing. The positions of billions of knobs together decide “after seeing these blocks, which block comes next?”

How it actually works

Each parameter is just a number (also called a weight). Training uses a method called “backpropagation” to work out which way to turn each knob, and how far.

The B in “7B” stands for billion: 7 billion parameters. The model's file size is roughly the number of parameters × the bytes each number takes up.

Common myth

“More parameters always means smarter.” Data quality, training methods and the later fine-tuning matter just as much. New small models often beat old big ones. And more parameters also means more expensive and slower to run.

PICTURE 5 · CONTEXT WINDOW

Its desk is only so big

Your chat history and any files you paste all have to be spread out on this desk. When the desk is full, the oldest stuff gets pushed off, and it “forgets.”

Think of it like…

An open-book test where your desk only fits a few pages. No matter how many books are on the shelf, anything not open on the desk is invisible.

How it actually works

The window is measured in tokens. It holds: the system instructions + the whole chat so far + any files you pasted + the answer it's writing right now.

The model itself remembers nothing. Every time you send a message, the app hands it the entire history again. “Memory” features in different apps keep notes on the side and put them back on the desk when needed.

Common myth

“A big window means it remembers everything perfectly.” When it's stuffed full, details buried in the middle are easier to miss. Put the important instructions at the start or the end and you'll get steadier results.

PICTURE 6 · TEMPERATURE

One knob decides “safe” or “wild”

That's why asking the same question twice can get you different answers. The randomness is on purpose.

Think of it like…

Ordering food. Low temperature: you always get your usual. High temperature: sometimes you try something you've never ordered.

How it actually works

Temperature controls how “pointy” that probability table is. At low temperature the top pick wins almost every time. At high temperature the gaps shrink, so long shots can get picked too.

Another common switch is called top-p: it only picks from the top-ranked options that add up to a certain share, so the totally off-the-wall options are cut out.

Common myth

“Higher temperature means more creative and smarter.” Too high and it starts talking nonsense. And setting temperature to 0 still doesn't guarantee the exact same words every time; there are other sources of randomness in the system.

PICTURE 7 · FROM BOOKWORM TO ASSISTANT

First read everything, then learn to be an assistant

Steps ② and ③ teach it to understand the question, answer properly, and know where the lines are.

Think of it like…

A med school grad knows a ton but can't treat patients yet. Residency teaches how to examine someone, how to talk to patients, and what you should never say.

How it actually works

Pretraining uses the vast majority of the computing power. Instruction tuning: show it lots of human-written “question → good answer” examples. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF): have it write several answers to the same question, let people rank them, then train it to lean toward the higher-rated style.

Tone, formatting, when to say no, and safety limits mostly take shape in these two steps. Some approaches have an AI do the scoring based on a written set of principles. Anthropic's Constitutional AI is one example.

Common myth

“Fine-tuning is how you pour new knowledge in.” These two steps mostly change behavior and style. Most of what it knows still comes from pretraining.

PICTURE 8 · HALLUCINATIONS

Sounding right isn't the same as being right

Names, numbers, dates and quotes are where it slips up most. For anything important, check the original source.

Think of it like…

A super smooth talker who never says “I don't know.” Hit a test question they can't answer, and they'll still fill a whole page that sounds pretty convincing.

How it actually works

Its goal is to “sound smooth,” not to “be true.” Rare facts show up only a few times in training and are stored fuzzily in its parameters, but it can still produce fluent sentences about them.

Ways to lower the risk: give it the original material and have it answer only from that; have it search the web and show its sources, then open them yourself and check. Asking it “are you sure?” doesn't help much.

Common myth

“Hallucination is a small bug that'll be fixed soon.” Newer models do hallucinate less, but it comes from the next-word-guessing way it works, so it's hard to get rid of completely. Names, numbers, dates, quotes, and anything about law, medicine or money: always check.

The short version

  1. Chop text into blocks
  2. Read a huge amount of text and learn to guess the next block
  3. Store the patterns in billions of knobs
  4. Then teach it to be an assistant
  5. It talks really well, but checking is your job
